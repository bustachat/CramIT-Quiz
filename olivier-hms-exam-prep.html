Focus Area 1 — Health in an Australian & global context
🩺 The Health of Australians Topic 1 ▼

Defining health

  • World Health Organisation (WHO) — "a state of complete physical, mental & social wellbeing, not merely the absence of disease" (holistic, but idealistic)
  • Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) — presence or absence of disease & measurable risk factors (the medical model — easier to measure)
  • Five dimensions — physical, mental, emotional, social, spiritual
  • Definitions differ because health is subjective & relative — judged against your own past, others & expectations

Dynamic nature & epidemiology

  • Health continuum — health is dynamic, constantly changing with illness, injury & environment
  • Epidemiology — the study of the causes & distribution of health conditions in populations, used to improve health
  • Uses — track diseases, stop outbreaks, plan & fund services
  • Key indicators — mortality, morbidity, infant mortality, life expectancy

Key measurement terms

  • Morbidity — level of ill health in a person or population
  • Mortality — the number of deaths in a population
  • Prevalence — total existing cases at a point in time
  • Incidence — rate of new cases over a set period
  • Burden of disease — gap between current health and an ideal, disease-free old age
  • DALY (disability-adjusted life year) — one year of healthy life lost to illness or early death

Life expectancy & causes

  • Life expectancy ≈ 83 yrs overall (♀ 85, ♂ 81)
  • Leading mortality — cardiovascular disease (CVD), coronary heart disease, dementia, lung cancer
  • Leading morbidity — mental illness, back pain, asthma, arthritis, diabetes
  • Risky-behaviour drivers — low socio-economic status (SES), culture & peer influence, rural isolation
  • Health inequities — shaped by social, economic & environmental factors

Determinants of Health

Inner layers (age, sex) can't be changed; outer layers (work, education, housing) can — and drive most inequity.

First Nations' health

  • Life expectancy gap of ~8 years (ABS, 2020–2023)
  • Chronic conditions are more prevalent and appear younger
  • Shaped by colonisation, connection to Country, education & employment, housing & food security
  • Actions: Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services, rural outreach, telehealth, school health promotion

Global context & ageing

  • Australia is an OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) member — high life expectancy, low smoking, strong immunisation
  • But mid-level for obesity & alcohol, fewer hospital beds per capita than Japan/Germany
  • About 1 in 6 Australians (~16%) are over 65 — projected to reach ~22% only by the 2060s
  • Healthy ageing = maintaining physical, social & mental function → strains hospitals, pensions, housing

Health inequities — rural & remote Australians

  • Data: life expectancy is ~1–2 years lower in regional areas and up to ~7 years lower in remote areas than in major cities (AIHW) — the gap widens with remoteness
  • Determinants interacting: distance → fewer GPs/specialists → delayed diagnosis; lower incomes + fewer jobs → worse housing/food security; isolation → less mental-health support
  • Causes: poorer access to and use of health services, lifestyle factors, and socio-economic disadvantage (education, employment)
  • Actions: telehealth, rural outreach clinics, incentives for rural GPs, mobile health services

Gender & risky health behaviours

  • Masculinity norms — social pressure to appear tough can push males toward smoking, heavy drinking or violence
  • Beauty standards — pressure to conform can push females toward unhealthy dieting, smoking (as appetite control) or excessive weight-control practices
  • Both are socio-cultural determinants — a strong link for an "explain the sociological causes" question
⚡ Exam Tip Don't confuse prevalence (all existing cases) with incidence (new cases only). Extended questions on health inequity want the determinants (rainbow) linked to a vulnerable group and a concrete action to close the gap. Remember the "one other group" question can ask about rural/remote, low-SES, CALD, disability or older people — rural/remote is the safest one to know in depth since it's the syllabus's own example.

🏥 Australia's Healthcare System Topic 2 ▼

Who runs it

  • Role: safe, affordable care for citizens/residents with equity for vulnerable groups
  • Government — Federal (Medicare, the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme [PBS], policy), State (hospitals, emergency), Local (health promotion, water, waste)
  • NGOs — private (e.g. BUPA) and not-for-profit (e.g. Beyond Blue)
  • Government = structure, funding & regulation; NGOs = personalised, community support

Funding & consumption

  • Medicare funded by the Medicare levy (+ surcharge for high earners); out-of-pocket gaps remain
  • Private health insurance funds private hospitals, easing the public load
  • Complementary care (acupuncture, yoga, herbal, massage; health apps & naturopathy) — used as prevention, treatment or alongside medical treatment
  • Critical consumption — trust sources with credentials, cited data & transparency (AIHW, WHO); check products against the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA)
  • Healthcare vs prevention: most of the health budget funds treating illness after it happens — public health/prevention gets only a small share (roughly 1–2%), even though prevention is usually more cost-effective long-term

Future opportunities

  • Rural & remote: expanding telehealth, incentivising rural placements, mobile/outreach services
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples: more Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services, culturally safe care embedded system-wide
  • People with disability: full NDIS rollout, better physical/digital accessibility of mainstream services

Current & emerging challenges

  • Rising wait times for GP appointments and emergency departments as demand grows
  • Workforce shortages, especially GPs and specialists in rural/regional areas
  • Privatisation debate — moving services to private providers can speed access for some but risks equity for those who can't pay
  • Ageing population & rising chronic disease increase long-term demand and cost pressure on the system
⚡ Exam Tip Learn the three lenses — sustainability, access, equity — and be able to judge any scheme (or challenge, like privatisation, ageing, or rising chronic disease) against all three. Person-centred care = government + NGOs collaborating (e.g. Headspace, subsidised flu vaccinations).
💻 Technology & Data in Healthcare Topic 3 ▼

Digital health & new tech

  • Digital health — apps, wearables, data systems & telehealth to deliver/manage care
  • My Health Record — a digital summary of a person's history → less repeat testing, better coordination
  • ePrescriptions sent electronically; Healthdirect is a trusted symptom checker
  • AI — mental-health chatbots, drug discovery, predicting deterioration, admin automation

Big data

  • Databases (AIHW, Medicare, hospitals) track outbreaks & population trends
  • Reduce spending — cut unnecessary tests, flag at-risk patients early, find inefficiencies
  • Disease management — faster trials, personalised medicine, side-effect tracking
  • Privacy: comply with the Privacy Act 1988 & My Health Records Act 2012 — encryption, consent, audits

Focus Area 2 — Training for improved performance
📋 Exercise Assessment & Prescription Topic 1 ▼

Pre-exercise screening

  • Assesses readiness for activity & the need for medical clearance
  • Builds a picture of individual health history
  • Lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, stress) let the plan be personalised by age, fitness & limitations
  • Reduces the risk of injury or adverse health events in training

Building a program

  • Identify baseline fitness, then set SMART goals
  • Match exercise to the client's needs, preferences & limitations
  • Balance intensity, duration & frequency safely
  • Track adaptation and progression over time
  • Common tests — VO₂ max, beep test, 1RM (strength), skinfolds (body composition), Cooper 12-min run
  • Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test — shuttle runs to a beep at rising speed, with a short active-recovery jog between shuttles → measures aerobic fitness and repeated-effort capacity under fatigue (netball, football)
  • Wingate test — a maximal 30-second cycle sprint against fixed resistance → measures anaerobic power and fatigue index (sprint/power sports)

🏋️ Training Methods & Adaptations Topic 2 ▼

Anaerobic (high intensity, short)

  • Uses the ATP-PC & lactic acid systems — sprinting, weightlifting, short bursts
  • Anaerobic intervals — repeated hard efforts, short rests → speed & lactate tolerance
  • HIIT (high-intensity interval training) — intense bursts + recovery → anaerobic power & aerobic fitness
  • Plyometrics — jumping/bounding → explosive power (volleyball, netball, AFL)
  • Resistance — strength & power (rugby, rowing, combat)

Aerobic (sustained)

  • Improves cardiovascular endurance via the aerobic system
  • Continuous — 30+ min sustained → best for marathon/endurance
  • Fartlek — steady state + sprints → footballers, hockey (variable game demands)
  • Aerobic intervals — moderate intervals → rowers, triathletes
  • Circuit — rotating stations → endurance + strength together

Flexibility

  • Static — hold a stretch → best post-training for recovery
  • Dynamic — movement-based → best warm-up before a match (leg swings)
  • Ballistic — fast bouncing → advanced warm-ups, with caution
  • PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) — contract–relax → greatest range-of-motion (ROM) gains; rehab & elite

Strength & skill

  • Free/fixed weights — strength & muscular endurance
  • Bodyweight — functional & accessible (push-ups, planks)
  • Elastic bands — rehab & mobility (swimmers, older adults, F1 drivers)
  • Skill/tactical — drills & modified games build 'game sense' & decision-making

The principles of training

  • Progressive overload — gradually ↑ the load to force continued adaptation
  • Specificity — train the energy system & muscles the sport actually uses
  • Reversibility — stop training and adaptations are lost ("use it or lose it")
  • Variety — vary the stimulus to prevent staleness, plateaus & boredom
  • Individuality — tailor the program to the athlete's needs & rate of response
  • Overtraining & recovery — schedule rest so adaptation, not breakdown, occurs

Thresholds, warm-up & cool-down

  • Aerobic threshold — ~55–85% of max HR: the zone that drives endurance gains
  • Anaerobic threshold — where lactate accumulates faster than it can be cleared
  • Warm-up — raise HR & body temp, dynamic stretch, skill rehearsal → prevents injury
  • Cool-down — light activity returns the body to rest & clears waste (lactic acid)
  • SIT (sprint interval training) vs HIIT — sprint intervals are all-out with long rest; HIIT is 85–95% max heart rate (MHR) bursts

👥 Individual vs Group Sports Topic 3 ▼

Yearly program phases

  • Pre-season (preparatory, ~6–12 weeks; up to 2–4 months in some sports) — HIGH volume, moderate intensity; a general phase builds the fitness base, then a specific phase adds skills & tactics
  • In-season (competition, ~5–6 months) — ↓ volume, HIGH intensity; strategy & tactics, maintain fitness, peak for games & recover between them
  • Off-season (transition, ~1 month) — ~1 week total rest, then active recovery/cross-training; address weaknesses & rehab injuries
  • Peaking — temporary optimal performance for the key event
  • Tapering — reduce volume/intensity 1–2 wks before → repairs microtears, tops up glycogen, cuts fatigue

Sub-phases & psychology

  • Macrocycle — the full annual plan; mesocycle — a 4–8 week block; microcycle — a 7–10 day block (the most detailed level)
  • Goal setting — personal (individual) vs shared/cohesion (team)
  • Mental imagery, self-talk, relaxation reduce anxiety & sharpen focus
  • Arousal regulation — calm for golf, high energy for sprints; teams use chants or slow breathing

Why tapering works — metabolic adaptation

  • Aerobic adaptations (mitochondrial & capillary density, haemoglobin) decline slowly once training eases
  • So a large early cut in volume is safe — fitness is retained while accumulated fatigue clears
  • Anaerobic adaptations fade faster → strength athletes use shorter, step tapers
  • Intensity stays high throughout the taper; only volume drops

Inverted-U hypothesis — arousal vs performance

A = Under-aroused: low motivation, poor focus B = Optimal: peak performance C = Over-aroused: tense, errors

Arousal & anxiety

  • Arousal — physiological activation & readiness (not the same as anxiety)
  • Fine motor skills (golf putt, archery) → low arousal is optimal
  • Gross motor skills (weightlifting, tackling) → high arousal is optimal
  • Trait anxiety — a stable personality tendency to feel anxious before every game
  • State anxiety — situation-specific nerves (e.g. only before a grand final)

Psychological strategies

  • Mental rehearsal / imagery — visualise success at performance speed
  • Relaxation — meditation, progressive muscle relaxation, centred breathing
  • Concentration — focus on the process (the next action), not the outcome
  • Goal setting — SMART goals; personal (individual) vs shared/cohesion (team)

Warm-up phases (in order)

  • Phase 1 — General — light jog 5–10 min → raise heart rate & body temperature
  • Phase 2 — Stretching — dynamic stretches → increase ROM, reduce injury risk
  • Phase 3 — Callisthenics — sport-specific conditioning exercises
  • Phase 4 — Skill rehearsal — movements & drills specific to the game

Factors that influence how strategies & tactics are applied

  • Nature of the sport & its rules/structure — set the boundaries for what strategies are even possible
  • Skill level & experience — advanced tactics need players who can execute them under pressure
  • Environmental & game conditions — weather, surface, altitude force in-game adjustments (e.g. shorter passes in wind/rain)
  • Opposition strengths & weaknesses — scouting shapes which tactics a coach chooses
  • Psychological readiness & team cohesion — a strategy only works if the team trusts it and each other
  • Communication & feedback — signals, calls and in-game coaching adjust tactics live
  • Physical conditioning & fatigue — tactics simplify as players tire late in a game

Role of technology in improving performance

  • Training innovations — resistance bands, reaction lights, altitude chambers, force plates, virtual/VR training simulations → sharpen a specific physical or perceptual quality
  • Equipment advances — carbon-fibre running shoes, aerodynamic helmets, smart textiles, anti-gravity treadmills → reduce resistance/injury load or allow earlier-stage training
  • Recording & monitoring — GPS trackers & wearables (load, speed, heart rate), video analysis (technique correction), live data dashboards (real-time coaching decisions)
  • Considerations — cost and access can widen the gap between elite and grassroots athletes; data can be misread without a coach's expertise
⚡ Exam Tip The classic 12-marker compares a yearly program for an individual vs a group athlete. The single biggest difference is competition frequency: a marathon runner tapers ~2 weeks for one annual race; a soccer team peaks every week and can only use a short mini-taper. For the 5-marker, apply a different psychological strategy to each (individual = imagery + centred breathing; team = shared goals + group relaxation).
😴 Sleep, Nutrition & Hydration Topic 4 ▼

Sleep

  • 8–10 hours recommended for adolescents & athletes
  • Plan consistent sleep/wake times; lighter training when sleep-deprived
  • Avoid screens before bed; use a calming nightly routine
  • Mechanism: deep sleep releases growth hormone (muscle repair); poor sleep raises cortisol → inflammation & injury risk (extra sleep improved basketball shooting ~9%)
  • Why: enhances focus, coordination, muscle repair & reaction time → safer movement, fewer injuries

Nutrition

  • Balanced diet — carbs (energy), protein (repair), fats, micronutrients
  • Fuel before & after training; carb-rich before, protein after
  • Regular eating times; anti-inflammatory foods (berries, leafy greens, omega-3) aid recovery
  • Why: maintains energy, supports muscle growth/repair, lowers injury risk

Hydration

  • 2–3 L water/day, more in heat or intense sessions; electrolytes for long/high-intensity work
  • ~500 mL water ~4 hrs before, ~300 mL 10–15 min before performance; drink before, during & after
  • Monitor — pale-yellow urine = hydrated; watch thirst, dry mouth & fatigue
  • Also maintains synovial fluid that lubricates joints for smoother movement
  • Why: maintains blood flow, reduces cramps, prevents overheating, improves concentration

Nutrient timing & glycemic index (GI)

  • Before (3–4 hr) — low-GI carbs (oats, grainy bread), moderate protein, low fat/fibre → slow, sustained energy
  • During (>60 min) — high-GI carbs, ~30–60 g/hr (gels, banana, sports drink) → fast glucose
  • After — high-GI carbs + protein → refill glycogen & repair muscle

Carbohydrate loading

  • Who — endurance events lasting >90 minutes only
  • How — raise carbohydrate to ~7–12 g/kg of body weight per day
  • When — across the 36–72 hours before the event
  • Must also taper training, or it won't work — boosts performance ~2–3%

Key nutrients

  • Electrolytes (Na, K, Ca, Mg) — lost in sweat; depletion causes muscle cramps
  • Iron — makes haemoglobin for O₂ transport; deficiency → early fatigue (esp. female & endurance athletes)
  • Low-GI foods — oats, grainy bread, lentils (slow release)
  • High-GI foods — gels, bananas, white bread (fast release)

RED-S — Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport

  • Occurs when energy intake is too low for the athlete's training demands
  • Causes hormonal disruption, impaired bone health & poor recovery
  • Affects female athletes most (studies suggest ~25–60% at risk)
  • Managed by matching energy intake to expenditure, with specialist support

Supplements — protein & creatine

  • Protein powder — convenient extra protein for repair/growth; benefit is real for athletes with high demands, but whole food already meets most needs — no benefit beyond the body's absorption limit
  • Creatine — boosts the ATP-PCR system's phosphate stores → more repeated short, powerful efforts (sprints, lifts); can cause water retention & weight gain, evidence is weaker for endurance events

Supplements — caffeine & micronutrients

  • Caffeine — a CNS stimulant; ↓ perceived effort, delays fatigue in endurance events; but can raise heart rate, disrupt sleep and cause dependency/anxiety with overuse
  • Micronutrients as supplements — iron and vitamin D correct a diagnosed deficiency effectively; taken without one, they add little benefit and can be harmful in excess
  • Overall: supplements work best alongside a good diet, not instead of one, and should be used under professional/medical guidance
⚡ Exam Tip Every answer here should end in a mechanism: sleep → growth hormone → protein synthesis → repair; dehydration → ↓ blood volume → less O₂ to muscles → fatigue. "It's important" scores nothing without the why. For nutrition: low-GI before, high-GI during/after, and carb loading only works with a taper.
🩹 Biomechanics, Recovery & Injury Topic 5 ▼

Biomechanics & recovery

  • Biomechanics — how muscles, bones, tendons & ligaments produce movement
  • Prevents injury & corrects form by analysing technique (e.g. stride length)
  • Recovery: cool-down (lowers HR, clears lactic acid), hydrotherapy (cold ↓ inflammation, hot ↑ blood flow), relaxation (↓ stress, better sleep)
  • Tech: wearables/GPS, smart gyms, VR skill practice, video analysis, live data dashboards

Types of injury

  • Direct — from external contact (ball, bat, person); indirect — internal force (sprains, strains)
  • Soft tissue — muscles, ligaments, fascia (tears, sprains, contusions, abrasions)
  • Hard tissue — bones/teeth (fractures, dislocations)
  • Overuse — repetitive stress (tendonitis, bursitis) from poor programming or insufficient rest

Assessment & soft-tissue management

  • TOTAPS — Talk, Observe, Touch, Active & Passive movement, Skills test
  • Sequence: DRSABCD (Danger, Response, Send for help, Airway, Breathing, CPR, Defibrillation — the crisis check) → STOP (Stop, Talk, Observe, Prevent — on-field) → TOTAPS (complete)
  • RICER for soft tissue — Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation, Referral
  • Ice ≈ 20 min every 2 hrs to reduce swelling & pain

Hard tissue, rehab & return

  • Dislocation — don't relocate; immobilise, ice, seek help
  • Fracture — immobilise (splint/sling), control bleeding, call emergency; RICER only after clearance
  • Rehab — progressive mobilisation → graduated exercise → modified training; cold early, heat later
  • Return-to-play — medical clearance, functional testing, monitor re-injury; physically and psychologically ready

RICER — soft-tissue management

Hard-tissue injuries

Rehabilitation stages (in order)

  • Progressive mobilisation — gently restore range of movement (active by the athlete, or passive by a practitioner); not during the acute inflammatory phase, and stay pain-free
  • Graduated exercise — static & PNF stretching, then conditioning using overload to rebuild strength and prevent muscle atrophy
  • Total body fitness — rebuild all muscle groups & energy systems (pool work, cycling ergometer, light jogging, weights)
  • Training — build sport-specific skills back to pre-injury level; complete once a full session is done pain-free

Return-to-play & ethics

  • Cold early, heat later — cold limits internal bleeding/swelling; heat (once bleeding stops) boosts blood flow & relaxes muscle for chronic tightness
  • Readiness indicators — elasticity, strength, mobility, pain-free, and balance on the injured limb
  • Psychological readiness — confidence rebuilt via sport-specific tests (e.g. beep test) & a sports psychologist
  • Ethics — painkillers mask pain (a damage warning) → further injury & prolonged healing; beware pressure to participate

Drug use — health & ethical considerations

  • Performance-enhancing drugs (steroids, stimulants, blood doping) can carry serious health risks — heart strain, hormonal disruption, dependency
  • Painkillers let an athlete play through injury but mask the body's damage warning → risk of a worse, prolonged injury
  • Ethical issue: drug use undermines fair competition and pressures other athletes to use them too, just to keep up

Drug testing

  • The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) sets the banned-substance list and testing rules used across sport
  • A Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) lets an athlete use an otherwise-banned medication for a genuine diagnosed medical condition, if approved in advance
  • Testing happens both in and out of competition; a positive test without an approved TUE can mean a competition ban
⚡ Exam Tip Know the acronyms cold — TOTAPS, DRSABCD, RICER — and the order they're used in. Never relocate a dislocation. Return-to-play answers must include both physical (functional testing) and psychological readiness. For drug use, cover all three angles the syllabus wants: health implications, ethics, and drug testing — not just painkillers.

✍️ Written Answer Scaffolds

These scaffolds match the three answer bands in the real HSC paper. Written responses reward structure, linking a fact to a consequence, and — in the longer bands — a clear judgement.

Short Answer — 2–4 marks (Section II)

These are single-part questions. The command verb and the mark value tell you exactly how much to write. Match the depth to the marks — one clear point per mark.

1

Read the verb first

Outline = state the main features briefly. Describe = state + add characteristics/detail. Explain = state + say how/why (the mechanism). The verb sets the ceiling on your marks.

📝 Verb → depthOutline (2 marks) = 1–2 precise sentences · Describe (3 marks) = the feature + its detail/example · Explain (4 marks) = the point + mechanism + a consequence.
2

One idea per mark

A 3-mark question needs three distinct, credit-worthy points. Don't repeat the same idea in different words — markers award separate points.

3

Always finish the chain

For Explain, never stop at naming a fact — link it to a mechanism and then a result. "Fact → because → therefore" scores the top mark.

Question (4 marks): Explain how one anaerobic training method could improve a 100-metre sprinter's performance. Plyometric training — repeated maximal jumps and bounds such as depth jumps and hurdle hops — overloads the fast-twitch muscle fibres and the stretch-shortening cycle (POINT + METHOD). Because sprinting relies almost entirely on the ATP-PC energy system for short, explosive efforts, this training is specific to the event's demands (MECHANISM). Over time it increases the sprinter's rate of force development and stride power (ADAPTATION), so they accelerate faster out of the blocks and reach a higher top speed (PERFORMANCE RESULT).
Extended Answer — 6–10 marks (Section II, usually parts a & b)

These questions almost always split into (a) and (b). Treat each part as its own question — the marks are awarded separately, so answer part (a) fully before starting (b).

1

Split by the marks

A "(a) 4 · (b) 6" question is really two answers. Give part (a) about a paragraph, and spend the most time on the higher-mark part (b).

📝 Rule of thumb2 marks ≈ 1–2 sentences · 4 marks ≈ a full paragraph with an example · 6 marks ≈ two developed points, each with an example.
2

Use a named example in every part

HMS rewards applied knowledge. Name a real sport, athlete type, scheme or technology in each part — a generic answer caps in the middle band.

3

Discuss / Analyse need a judgement

If the verb is Discuss or Analyse, description alone won't reach the top band. Weigh strengths against limitations and end with a judgement sentence.

📝 Judgement sentence"Overall, [strategy] is most effective when… but is limited by…, so it works best alongside…"
Question: (a) Outline the roles of the three levels of government in Australia's healthcare system. (3 marks) (b) Explain how Medicare supports both access and equity. (4 marks) (a) The federal government funds and regulates the system nationally, running Medicare and the PBS and setting health policy. State and territory governments deliver frontline services — they run public hospitals and emergency care. Local governments handle community-level health, such as immunisation programs, health promotion, and environmental health like clean water and waste. (Three distinct roles = 3 marks.) (b) Medicare supports ACCESS by providing universal care: any citizen or resident can see a bulk-billing GP or be treated in a public hospital regardless of their ability to pay, removing cost as a barrier to essential treatment. It supports EQUITY through the Medicare Safety Net, which gives extra financial help to people who need frequent care — often the chronically ill or low-income earners — so those with the greatest need receive the most support. By funding care through the income-based Medicare levy, the system spreads cost according to capacity to pay, further improving fairness. (Access + equity each explained with a specific mechanism = 4 marks.)
Extended Response — 12 marks (Section III, stimulus-based)

Section III has two 12-mark questions — one from Focus Area 1, one from Focus Area 2 — each with a stimulus (an infographic, data table or scenario). The single biggest marks-winner is referring explicitly to the stimulus throughout your answer.

1

Read the stimulus first — and use it

Underline 2–3 concrete details in the stimulus (a statistic, a scenario detail) and build your answer around them. The rubric explicitly rewards using the stimulus and relevant examples.

📝 Rubric wantsApply knowledge & understanding · apply critical thinking · use relevant examples/concepts/terms · present a logical, cohesive response.
2

INTRO — define + address the framing

Define the key terms and directly name the question's framing — "to what extent" or "evaluate" — so the marker sees you're answering the actual question.

3

BODY — 2–3 points, each tied back to the stimulus

Each paragraph = one point, developed with a mechanism and an example, then explicitly linked to a detail in the stimulus.

4

JUDGEMENT — answer "to what extent" / "evaluate"

Don't sit on the fence. State how far the strategy/advice helps, and name what limits it.

5

CONCLUSION — 2 sentences that resolve the framing

Stimulus: The town of "Riverton" — 31% of youth overweight (state avg 24%), 1 in 4 in high psychological distress, 38% of households in the lowest income bracket, only 29% play weekly sport, and just 0.6 GPs per 1,000 people. Question: To what extent could SDG 3 (Good Health and Wellbeing) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) assist in improving the health status of young people in Riverton? INTRODUCTION The Sustainable Development Goals are global targets that guide health and equity. Riverton's data shows clear disadvantage — high obesity, psychological distress, low income and poor healthcare access. SDG 3 and SDG 10 could substantially improve young people's health, but only to the extent that local barriers of income and service access are also addressed. SDG 3 — GOOD HEALTH AND WELLBEING SDG 3 targets universal health coverage and the reduction of non-communicable disease and poor mental health. Applied to Riverton, it justifies investment in the GP shortage (0.6 vs 1.2 per 1,000) and youth mental-health services, directly targeting the "1 in 4 in high distress" figure. Programs promoting physical activity would address the low 29% sport-participation rate, reducing the 31% overweight figure over time. To this extent SDG 3 is highly relevant, because it maps onto Riverton's specific health deficits. SDG 10 — REDUCED INEQUALITIES However, Riverton's problems are rooted in disadvantage: 38% of households are in the lowest income bracket, a socio-economic determinant that drives poor diet, inactivity and distress. SDG 10 addresses this by reducing income and social inequality — for example, subsidised sport, free school-based health programs, and better-funded local services. Without tackling this underlying inequality, SDG 3 health services alone would be underused, so the two goals are most powerful applied together. JUDGEMENT The goals could improve Riverton's youth health to a significant extent, but not fully on their own. SDG 3 treats the visible health outcomes while SDG 10 addresses the socio-economic cause; the improvement depends on implementing both, and on sustained local funding and community engagement. CONCLUSION SDG 3 and SDG 10 are highly applicable to Riverton because its poor health outcomes are tied to measurable inequality. Used together — services plus equity — they offer a realistic path to improving young people's health, provided the strategies are properly resourced and community-led.
